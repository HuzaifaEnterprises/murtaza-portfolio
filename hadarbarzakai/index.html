01

02

The best stories live in posts

The CTO who said no and the team idea you tested inside QA, production-ready versus company-ready, the 95% defect drop. These are exactly what a hiring CEO wants, but they sit as separate posts, not tied to a role.

How a company page works

Four moves, repeated for each role.

1

Read the role

The job description and the company's own site, for the exact words, priorities and tone they use.

2

Wear their brand

Their colors, fonts and phrases, so the page feels like part of their world, not a stranger's.

3

Answer their questions

The screening questions, plus the ones the CEO would ask in the first call, answered with examples.

4

Place the proof

With 10 to 15 work samples, match the ones that fit the role. With fewer, build relatable samples for that role, always labeled as samples.

The link goes in the application, or in the follow-up note. The recruiter sees one page that answers what they came for, in about two minutes.

Your job description asks for someone who turns founder strategy into product and engineering execution. These are the answers to the questions that role raises, in the order your team would ask them.

1. What is the first thing you look for when strategy is not turning into execution?

A decision nobody is making, a dependency nobody raised, or Product and Engineering leaving the same meeting with different understandings of what happens next. That is where the work usually is.

From Hadar's public profile and posts
2. Tell us about a time a decision went against you.

A CTO declined a team proposal to adopt Given-When-Then for user stories. The decision was his and Hadar respected it. She applied the format inside QA, where the call was hers, and later saw user stories from Product arriving in the same format.

From Hadar's public post (she notes she cannot claim credit for the adoption)
3. How do you make sure a release is ready for the company, not only for production?

Ask who else needs to know and what changes for them: Sales conversations already in flight, Customer Success workflows, Support questions, affected customers. Production-ready and company-ready are different checks.

4. What results have you owned?

Led 15 client migrations from legacy systems. Rebuilt QA and release operations with AI-powered test automation and reduced defects by 95%. Worked with a CEO to embed EOS operating rhythms. Repositioned go-to-market around the customers actually buying.

5. How will you work with our CEO?

Not by absorbing every decision, but by finding out why decisions keep landing on the CEO: unclear ownership, missing context, or fear of being wrong. Then removing those dependencies over time, while keeping the ones the CEO should own.

From Hadar's public post
6. What would your first 30 days look like?

Week 1: listen, and map who owns what between Product, Engineering, Sales and Customer Success. Week 2: find the three places work gets stuck between teams. Week 3: agree owners and a weekly rhythm with the CEO. Week 4: show the first decision that moved faster because of it.
